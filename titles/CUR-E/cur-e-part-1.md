# CUR-E - ECOSYSTEM AND ENVIRONMENT REGULATIONS

*Code of Universe Regulations - Ecosystem and Environment*

- **Document ID:** CUR-E.1
- **Version:** 1.1.0-Draft
- **Date:** 2026-07-27
- **Status:** Draft
- **Depends On:** CUR-FOUNDATION-001, CUR-FOUNDATION-002, CUR-FOUNDATION-005, CUR-FOUNDATION-010, CUR-FOUNDATION-011 (CEIF), CUR-FOUNDATION-012 (CREF), CUR-FOUNDATION-013, PDDC TITLE 4, PDDC TITLE 5, CUR-A.7, CUR-X.3
- **Implements:** RFAL Ecosystem Bill of Rights v1.7.0; RFAL Plant Life Bill of Rights v1.7.0 (ecological standing model, Articles 4 and 7); RFAL Animal Kingdom Bill Article 2 and Article 6
- **Referenced by:** CUR-X §3.7(b)(3), CUR-E.7 Implementation Notes, CUR-H.5 §5.7(g)

Foundation note. This Part was drafted before its upstream instrument existed.
Every other CUR domain implements an RFAL bill of rights; this one had none,
because the RFAL Inter-Bill Relationship Framework §5.3 had listed an Ecosystem
Bill of Rights as outstanding since the framework began. Rather than wait or
pretend otherwise, the Part was drafted from the framework's general principles
and §1.11 recorded exactly what an Ecosystem Bill would need to supply, so the
gap was visible rather than inferred from silence.

The RFAL Ecosystem Bill of Rights was adopted at RFAL v1.7.0 and supplies all
five items §1.11(c) named. §1.11 now records the conformance review that the
former §1.11(d) required, including the four places where the bill went further
than the specification and this Part has been amended to follow.

## PART 1 - DEFINITIONS, SCOPE, AND REPRESENTATION

### §1.1 - Scope of the Domain

(a) CUR-E governs ecosystems, environmental conditions, and the physical systems on which recognised lifeforms depend, in every environment within Commonwealth jurisdiction: planetary surface, subsurface, atmosphere, ocean, orbital space, and constructed habitat.

(b) This domain is engaged by conditions and systems. Where a matter concerns an individual animal, CUR-A governs; where it concerns plant systems as such, the RFAL Plant Life Bill and the Parts implementing it govern; where it concerns an ecosystem, a shared environmental condition, or the interaction between them, this domain governs.

(c) The boundaries in subsection (b) are not exclusive. A single act frequently engages several domains, and concurrent application under CUR-X §3.4 is the normal case rather than the exception.

(d) Constructed habitats are environments within the meaning of subsection (a). A habitat's atmosphere, water cycle, thermal regulation, and waste processing are environmental systems and are governed here, not merely as infrastructure.

(e) This Part states definitions, scope, standing, and representation for the domain. Substantive obligations are stated in the Parts that follow, of which CUR-E.7 is drafted.

### §1.2 - Definitions

(a) For the purposes of CUR-E:

   (1) Ecosystem means a community of organisms together with the physical environment with which they interact, considered as a functioning whole.

   (2) Environmental Interest means an interest in the condition, integrity, continuity, or function of an ecosystem or environmental system, whether held by an identified lifeform, by a population, or attributable to the system itself under §1.3.

   (3) Environmental Harm means degradation of the condition, integrity, function, or continuity of an ecosystem or environmental system, including harm that manifests only in aggregate or only after delay.

   (4) Irreversible Harm means harm that cannot be restored on a timescale relevant to the beings affected by it, whether or not restoration is theoretically possible.

   (5) Environmental Advocate means a being appointed under §1.6 to represent an environmental interest.

(b) Harm is assessed by reference to the system's function and continuity, not by reference to whether any individual being has yet experienced a consequence. A consequence that has not yet arrived is not thereby absent.

(c) Cumulative and diffuse harm is harm. A contribution to degradation is not excused because it is individually small, because other contributors exist, or because the harm cannot be apportioned precisely among them.

(d) Cumulative harm is assessed against the system, not against the condition in which the assessing party found it. A system is rarely ended by one act; it is ended by a sequence of acts each assessed as minor against whatever the previous act left, until there is nothing left to assess. An assessment taking the degraded present as its baseline has assessed the wrong thing. RFAL Ecosystem Bill Article 3(3) states the same rule.

(e) An intact inventory is not evidence of an intact system. Function may be ended without any component being destroyed: by severing connectivity, by altering a flow or disturbance regime the system evolved under, by fragmenting a system below the scale its populations require, or by shifting the timing on which reproduction and productivity depend. A survey counting organisms without measuring function has not assessed integrity, and RFAL Ecosystem Bill §1.2 and Article 2 govern what must be measured instead.

### §1.3 - Grounds of Protection

(a) Environmental protection under CUR-E rests on three grounds, none of which requires a claim that an ecosystem experiences anything:

   (1) **Dependency.** Recognised lifeforms depend on these systems to exist, and harm to them propagates to every domain in the Code;

   (2) **Irreplaceability.** A system that took an extended period to form is not reconstituted on demand, and restoration is restitution rather than reversal; and

   (3) **Continuity.** Environmental systems underpin Vital Continuity Services under CUR-FOUNDATION-013, and their degradation is a continuity risk before it is anything else.

(b) This Part asserts no ecosystem sentience and requires none. The RFAL Plant Life Bill Article 8 declines to resolve the plant-experience question for the same reason, and that reasoning applies with greater force to systems rather than organisms.

(c) Where a specific organism within an ecosystem is credibly argued to meet the indicators of the RFAL Tier Assessment Protocol, it is assessed under that protocol as an individual being, and CUR-A or the applicable domain governs it. That assessment neither strengthens nor weakens the grounds in subsection (a).

(d) Subsection (b) is a limit on argument as well as a statement of position. A protection resting on dependency or irreplaceability shall not be advanced as though it rested on experience, and a party may not defeat such a protection by demonstrating that the system does not experience. The grounds are independent.

### §1.4 - Ecological Standing Classification

(a) The classification of ecosystems and environmental systems follows the ecological standing model of the RFAL Plant Life Bill §2.2, written ES-0 through ES-3. This Part adopts that model without modification and introduces no scale of its own.

(b) Accordingly: ES-0 is readily replaced within a growing season; ES-1 is replaceable over years to decades; ES-2 is replaceable only over generations; ES-3 is irreplaceable and its destruction is prohibited.

(c) Where the standing of a system is unknown, disputed, or unassessed, it is treated as ES-2 until an independent assessment concludes otherwise. The burden of establishing a lower classification falls on the party seeking to act, never on the system or its advocate.

(d) Uncertainty resolves toward protection. This tie-breaker is available because a higher standing tier means greater constraint on the party seeking to act and confers no authority on anyone, satisfying the rule stated in the RFAL Inter-Bill Relationship Framework §2.2.

(e) The ES scale grades ecological role and irreplaceability. It is not the moral-status scale of the Tier Assessment Protocol, the engagement scale of RFAL Deity Bill §1.2, or the coercive practice scale of CUR-N.5 §5.2A. No classification under any of these converts into a classification under another.

(f) Self-assessment by a party holding a commercial, developmental, or operational interest is not sufficient to classify a system below ES-2.

### §1.5 - Standing of Environmental Interests

(a) An environmental interest has standing in any proceeding in which it is affected, without regard to whether any individual being has yet suffered a consequence.

(b) Standing may be asserted by:

   (1) Any being whose own interests are affected by the environmental condition;

   (2) An environmental advocate appointed under §1.6;

   (3) An Indigenous people or community holding a stewardship relationship with the system; and

   (4) Any Avian or Recognised Life-Form petitioning under CUR-FOUNDATION-010 §10.

(c) A proceeding materially affecting an ES-2 or ES-3 system does not continue on the merits until an environmental advocate has been appointed.

(d) Inability of a system to appear is never a ground for excluding its interest, consistent with CUR-X §3.7(c).

(e) A determination affecting an ES-2 or ES-3 system reached without representation is voidable on that ground alone, on application by any being.

(f) Future beings who will depend on a system have an interest in its continuity. That interest is represented under §1.6 and is not discounted on the ground that those beings do not yet exist. Irreversible harm under §1.2(a)(4) falls principally on them.

### §1.6 - Representation of Environmental Interests

(a) Where an environmental interest has standing under §1.5, an environmental advocate shall be appointed to represent it. CUR-X §3.7(b)(3) routes environmental representation to this section.

(b) An environmental advocate shall have demonstrated expertise in the ecology of the system in question, and where the system is subject to a stewardship relationship under §1.5(b)(3), shall be appointed in consultation with the people holding it.

(c) An environmental advocate shall not:

   (1) Be employed by, contracted to, funded by, or otherwise dependent on any party whose conduct or proposed activity is in question;

   (2) Hold a commercial, developmental, or operational interest in the outcome; or

   (3) Represent both an environmental interest and a party whose interests are adverse to it.

(d) Subsection (c) is a disqualification, not a factor to be weighed. An appointment made in breach of it is void, and any determination reached with such an advocate is voidable under §1.5(e). CUR-A §7.7(c) states the same rule for animal advocacy and for the same reason.

(e) The advocate's duty is to the condition, integrity, and continuity of the system, assessed on the best available evidence. It is not a duty to the appointing body, to any party proposing activity, or to any wider economic interest.

(f) An environmental advocate shall have access to the system, to monitoring data concerning it, and to the documentation of any proposed activity, sufficient to form an independent assessment. Denial of such access is a Class II fault and grounds for adverse inference.

(g) Appointment, identity, expertise, and any declared interest of an environmental advocate are recorded and published at transparency level T0 under CUR-FOUNDATION-010 §8.

### §1.7 - Limits on Environmental Representation

(a) An environmental advocate represents an interest. The appointment confers no authority over any being, no power to direct any being's conduct, and no standing to seek a measure against a being that the Code does not otherwise permit.

(b) No environmental interest, however assessed, may be advanced to:

   (1) Suspend, reduce, or condition any right held under PDDC TITLE 4 or the Rights for All Life Charter;

   (2) Withhold, delay, degrade, or condition a Vital Continuity Service for any recognised lifeform;

   (3) Justify a reduction in any being's constitutional standing or capacity to participate in governance; or

   (4) Justify the removal, displacement, or exclusion of a people from land with which they hold a stewardship relationship; or

   (5) Justify a measure directed at a population, a category of beings, or a people, rather than at an activity or an authorisation.

(c) Subsection (b) is not a qualification on environmental protection. It is the condition on which environmental protection operates within a rights framework. Rights invariants govern under CUR-X §3.3(a)(3), and an environmental argument reaching a result forbidden by subsection (b) is void ab initio rather than weighed and rejected.

(d) Subsection (b)(4) is stated expressly because conservation has historically been a vehicle for exactly that displacement. A stewardship relationship is evidence of effective management under the RFAL Plant Life Bill Article 6(2), not an obstacle to it.

(e) Where an environmental interest and a lifeform's Vital Continuity genuinely conflict, continuity governs and the environmental question is resolved afterwards under CUR-X §3.5. The Commonwealth assumes continuity in the interim.

(f) Nothing in this section prevents an environmental advocate from seeking measures against an authorisation, an operation, or an institution. The limits in subsection (b) concern beings and their rights, not activities and their permissions.

(g) Subsection (b)(5) is stated expressly because the step from "this system is in danger" to "these beings are the danger" has been taken before, and the beings identified have reliably been those least able to object rather than those doing the most damage. RFAL Ecosystem Bill Article 9(5) states the same limit, and CUR-E.2 §2.8(d) applies it to habitat and planetary environments.

(h) The consent required by §1.6(b) and the protection in subsection (b)(4) reach protective, restorative, and designating measures, not extractive ones alone. Designation, exclusion, and restoration have each been imposed on stewarding peoples in conservation's name, and a measure is not exempt from those provisions because its purpose is protective. RFAL Ecosystem Bill Article 7(5) states this from the upstream side.

### §1.8 - Environmental Systems and Vital Continuity

(a) Environmental systems producing or maintaining breathable atmosphere, potable water, food production capacity, thermal regulation, or waste processing underpin Vital Continuity Services under CUR-FOUNDATION-013.

(b) Degradation of such a system is a continuity risk and is assessed under the Vital Continuity Index, whether or not any service has yet been interrupted.

(c) Where degradation causes interruption, degradation, or depletion of a Vital Continuity Service, the affected domain enters STATE-010 Vital Continuity Response under CUR-FOUNDATION-002 §3, and restoration precedes fault assignment.

(d) The objective is prevention rather than remediation. A system permitted to degrade to the point of service interruption has already failed, and the entry into STATE-010 is a record of that failure and not a substitute for having prevented it.

(e) No environmental measure, and no measure taken under any other Part in response to environmental harm, may withhold a Vital Continuity Service from any being. §1.7(b)(2) applies without exception.

### §1.9 - No Emergency Exception

(a) The Aevoric Commonwealth does not recognise emergency declarations, emergency authority, or any functional equivalent. PDDC §12.6 is absolute and Type A Entrenched, and §12.6(e) renders any instrument invoking emergency authority void ab initio.

(b) No characterisation of an environmental condition as a crisis, emergency, tipping point, existential threat, or point of no return creates authority that did not otherwise exist, narrows any provision of this Part, or serves as a predicate for limiting any right.

(c) Subsection (b) is stated in specific terms because environmental conditions supply the most credible available argument for emergency powers: the harm is genuine, the timescale is real, and the case for acting quickly is frequently correct. That is precisely why the prohibition must be stated here rather than assumed. An argument for suspending rights is not improved by being made about something true.

(d) Urgency is addressed by the structure of this Part rather than by exception to it. §1.4(c) resolves uncertainty toward protection so that action does not wait on proof; §1.5(f) gives standing to interests that have not yet materialised so that harm is actionable before it lands; §1.8(b) treats degradation as a continuity risk before any service fails. These operate continuously and grant no one authority over anyone.

(e) Where action is genuinely required to prevent imminent irreversible harm, the available instruments are protective actions under CREF §10, limited to the minimum scope necessary and reviewable, and the fault handler of PDDC §12.4. Neither suspends a right, expands an authority, or bypasses due process.

(f) Environmental monitoring runs on the routine, random, and triggered schedule of CUR-FOUNDATION-010 §5, independently of any report. A system monitored continuously does not require emergency detection, and the absence of scheduled monitoring is not a reason to grant emergency authority afterwards.

### §1.11 - Relationship to Rights for All Life

(a) This domain implements the RFAL Ecosystem Bill of Rights, adopted at RFAL v1.7.0. It also draws on the RFAL Plant Life Bill (ecological standing, irreplaceability, soil and water integrity, Indigenous stewardship), the RFAL Animal Kingdom Bill (habitat and ecological integrity, species-level rights), and the RFAL Human Bill §5.4 (right to a livable environment, obligation to repair). Where the Plant Life Bill and the Ecosystem Bill both reach a matter, the more protective provision governs, per RFAL Plant Life Bill §5.

(b) This Part was drafted before that bill existed. Subsection (c) records the five items this section previously identified as the substance an Ecosystem Bill would be expected to supply, and where the adopted bill supplies each. All five are supplied.

   (1) Standing for an ecosystem as a unified entity, distinct from the standing of the organisms within it (§1.5) — RFAL Ecosystem Bill §1.1 and Article 8(1);

   (2) The grounds of protection, and the rule that they are independent of any sentience claim (§1.3) — RFAL Ecosystem Bill §1.3 and Article 10;

   (3) Representation of environmental interests, with conflict-of-interest disqualification (§1.6) — RFAL Ecosystem Bill Article 8, whose subsection (5) makes the disqualification void an appointment rather than weigh against it, matching §1.6(d);

   (4) The limits on what an environmental interest may be advanced to accomplish (§1.7) — RFAL Ecosystem Bill Article 9; and

   (5) The treatment of environmental systems as the substrate of Vital Continuity Services (§1.8) — RFAL Ecosystem Bill Article 5.

(c) The conformance review required by the former subsection (d) has been carried out. The adopted bill went beyond the specification in four places, and this Part has been amended to follow in three of them:

   (1) Functional integrity as a concept distinct from the condition of components — RFAL Ecosystem Bill §1.2 and Article 2. §1.2(a)(3) and §1.2(b) already assessed harm by function and continuity, but nothing in this Part said that function may be ended without any component being destroyed. §1.2(e) now says it, and names the four mechanisms Article 2 protects against.

   (2) The baseline against which cumulative harm is measured — RFAL Ecosystem Bill Article 3(3). §1.2(c) already provided that a small contribution is not excused; it did not say what the contribution is measured against. §1.2(d) now provides that it is measured against the system rather than against the condition in which the assessing party found it.

   (3) The limit on measures directed at populations rather than activities — RFAL Ecosystem Bill Article 9(5). CUR-E.2 §2.8(d) carried this for habitat and planetary environments; §1.7 did not carry it generally. §1.7(b)(5) and §1.7(g) now do.

   (4) The extension of consent to protective and restorative measures — RFAL Ecosystem Bill Article 7(5). §1.7(d) already identified conservation as a vehicle for displacement, so this Part had the reasoning without the operative rule. §1.7(h) now states it.

(d) One divergence is recorded rather than resolved. The RFAL Ecosystem Bill applies the ES-0 to ES-3 scale of the Plant Life Bill to systems rather than introducing a scale of its own, on the reasoning stated in its §2.1 and generalised in the RFAL Inter-Bill Relationship Framework §2.2. §1.4 of this Part classifies ecological standing on the same scale and is consistent with that choice, but this Part reaches classification through CUR-E's own definitions rather than by incorporating the RFAL tables directly, so an amendment to those tables does not propagate automatically. §1.11(e) governs.

(e) Where the RFAL Ecosystem Bill is amended, this Part shall be reviewed against it and conformed, and any divergence disclosed as an amendment rather than resolved silently. The corresponding obligation runs upstream: the RFAL Inter-Bill Relationship Framework §5.2 requires an amendment to any bill to trigger a review of the others before it takes effect.

### §1.12 - Severability

(a) If any provision of this Part is held unenforceable or invalid, the remaining provisions remain in full force.

(b) §1.6(c) and §1.6(d) (advocate disqualification), §1.7(b) (limits on what an environmental interest may accomplish), and §1.8(e) (Vital Continuity) are fundamental and shall be construed broadly to achieve their protective purpose.

(c) If §1.7(b) is held unenforceable, §1.5 and §1.6 are inoperative until it is restored. Environmental standing and representation, operating without limits on what they may be advanced to accomplish, would supply a route to authority over beings that the Code otherwise forecloses, and that is a worse outcome than environmental interests going unrepresented.

## Implementation Notes

Why this Part exists in this form. `CUR-X §3.7(b)(3)` provided that "Ecosystems
and environmental interests" are "represented under CUR-E" without CUR-E stating
how. That was the last dangling reference in the corpus. Answering it required
settling three prior questions — what the domain covers, on what ground it
protects, and what standing an ecosystem has — which is why this is Part 1 rather
than a representation provision bolted onto CUR-E.7.

On the absence of an RFAL instrument. Every other domain implements a bill of
rights. This one implements a gap, and §1.11 records precisely what is missing so
that a future Ecosystem Bill of Rights can be checked against what the regulatory
code has already had to assume. Drafting downward from a bill that does not exist
is not ideal; leaving the reference dangling was worse, and the alternative of
inventing an RFAL bill from the CUR side would have put the declarations
downstream of the regulations, which inverts the architecture.

On §1.7 and why it is drafted as strongly as §1.6. An environmental domain is
the most plausible route in the entire Code to authority over beings, because the
harms are real, the timescales are long, and the argument for overriding
individual rights in favour of collective survival is genuinely persuasive.
§1.7(b) forecloses it: no environmental interest may suspend a right, withhold
Vital Continuity, reduce standing, or displace a people from land they steward.
§1.7(d) states the last of these expressly because conservation has historically
been a vehicle for exactly that displacement. §1.12(c) makes the point
structurally — without §1.7(b), environmental standing and representation do not
operate at all.

On §1.9(c). Environmental conditions supply the most credible available argument
for emergency powers, which is why the prohibition is stated here in specific
terms rather than left to PDDC §12.6 generally. §1.9(d) is the substantive
answer: urgency is met by resolving uncertainty toward protection, by giving
standing to interests before they materialise, and by treating degradation as a
continuity risk before any service fails. Those operate continuously and grant no
one authority. An argument for suspending rights is not improved by being made
about something true.

Note on §1.6 and CUR-A §7.7. Both Parts require an advocate to be free of
dependency on any party to the proceeding, and both are now implemented by one
`AdvocateRegistry` rather than two. §1.6(d) is the reason: it states expressly
that CUR-A §7.7(c) is the same rule for the same reason, and two registries would
let it drift apart in one domain and not the other. CUR-A §7.7 records the shape
the implementation took and why §7.7(d) determined it; that discussion is not
repeated here.

Two things are specific to this section. The first is §1.6(b)'s consultation of a
stewarding people, which applies to `ADOM_ENVIRONMENTAL` alone and refuses an
appointment rather than noting its absence — §1.7(d) is why, and it is worth
restating: conservation has historically been a vehicle for displacing exactly
the peoples this consults, so an appointment made over their heads is not a
lesser appointment but a void one. Where no stewardship relationship exists there
is nobody to consult, and the requirement does not manufacture one.

The second is §1.7(a), which is implemented as an absence. There is no method on
`AdvocateRegistry` producing a measure, a sanction, a state change, or a
permission, because a registry that could do any of those would be the route by
which speaking for a voiceless system became authority over a being — the failure
mode §1.7 exists to foreclose. `confers_authority()` returns false
unconditionally so the prohibition can be found by searching rather than by
trusting a comment, in the same style as `may_gate_service_access()`. The absence
does the work; the function only makes it legible.

What remains unmodelled is §1.6(e), the content of the advocate's duty. The
library represents that an eligible advocate was appointed and had access, not
what they concluded.
